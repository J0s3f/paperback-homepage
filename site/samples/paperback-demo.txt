You are reading the demonstration file that the sample pages on the homepage
restore. If your decoder produced exactly these bytes, everything worked: the
scanner-side reader, the error correction and the recovery blocks all did their
job. The name stored on the pages is paperback-demo.txt, and the modification
time is fixed so that every run of the sample generator produces the very same
pages.

What is paperback.rs?

paperback.rs is a command-line program that stores files on paper. It is a Rust
port of PaperBack 1.10, the classic data-backup program by Oleh Yuschuk. You give
it a file, it renders that file as dense grids of black and white dots on ordinary
sheets, and you print those sheets. Later, you photograph or scan the sheets and
the program reads the file back. Paper is cheap, stable and readable without any
special hardware, so a box of printed pages makes a surprisingly durable archive.

The pages carry more than the raw payload. Each block of data has a checksum, and
the pages carry recovery blocks as well, so a smudge, a staple or a torn corner
does not have to be fatal. The reader tries several ways of sampling a block and
can rebuild a limited number of lost blocks from the recovery data. It also
prints a quality overlay that shows block by block how well a scan was read.

Formats and compatibility

The encoder writes PNG, BMP and PDF pages; BMP is the one format the original
PaperBack opens directly. The decoder reads PNG, JPEG and BMP images as well as
PDF documents, in any order, and it accepts scans and photographs, not just clean
renders. Pages written by this program are meant to be read by the original
program, and pages written by the original program are read back here: the two
are interoperable in both directions.

How to test the web decoder

The homepage runs the actual Rust library as WebAssembly in your browser. Nothing
is uploaded: your images stay on your machine. To try it, scroll to the decoder
section and do one of two things.

First, the two sample buttons load a page that is already part of the site. The
clean sample, sample-page.png, decodes to a file that you can download as
paperback-demo.txt. Its quality overlay should be green nearly everywhere. The
damaged sample, sample-damaged.png, has small squares wiped out of the dot field;
its overlay shows those squares in red and orange, yet the file still restores
because the recovery blocks fill the holes back in.

Second, you can drop your own page images or a PDF straight onto the drop zone.
The browser decodes whatever image format it understands, or the library reads
the bytes itself when the browser cannot. You can select several pages of one
backup at once, in any order; the decoder assembles them into a single file.

Reading the overlay

Green means a block was read cleanly. Yellow and orange mean the reader needed
more correction before it trusted the block. Red marks a block it could not read
at all, or one that had to be rebuilt from the recovery data. The overlay is shown
even when the whole file cannot be restored, because that is exactly when you want
to see which page went wrong.

Practical notes for scanning

Scan at 300 dots per inch or better, in grayscale, with no sharpening and no
automatic rotation. Keep the whole sheet inside the frame, including the hint text
along the bottom edge, and keep the page flat. If a page reads badly, rescanning it
at a higher resolution usually fixes more than any amount of post-processing. You
do not have to name the files in any particular way and you do not have to feed the
pages back in order.
